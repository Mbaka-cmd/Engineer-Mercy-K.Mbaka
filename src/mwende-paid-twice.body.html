The Day Mwende Paid Twice

An engineering story about M-Pesa, callbacks, and the difference between receiving a payment and recording it correctly.

Fictional scenario. Mwende is an invented character used to illustrate a realistic failure. This is not an account of an incident in any real school or in the School Management SaaS.

At 10:14 a.m., Mwende sent her school fees.

She was in Form Three. Her mother had sent the money that morning, and Mwende had one job: pay the balance before classes resumed.

Her phone vibrated. Payment successful.

She waited for the school portal to update. Nothing changed. The balance was still there. She refreshed the page, logged out, logged back in. Still unpaid.

She did what any worried student would do. She tried again.

At 10:17 a.m., a second confirmation arrived. This time the portal updated.

Now Mwende had a new problem. The school’s system showed one payment. Her phone showed two. Her mother had sent enough for one.

And the bursar, looking at a ledger with one entry and a parent insisting she had been charged twice, needed to know what had happened.

Two questions hiding inside one incident

At first glance this looks like a payment failure. But there are two separate questions:

  • Did the mobile-money provider process the transaction?
  • Did our application record that transaction correctly?

They are not the same question. A payment integration is a conversation between systems. One asks, another processes, and a confirmation travels back. Along the way a response can arrive late, a connection can drop, or the same confirmation can be delivered more than once.

Two failures that look alike

A confirmation that never arrives, or is never processed. The money moved, but the school’s records say it did not. The payer sees an unpaid balance and, reasonably, pays again. Mwende’s morning looks like this: the first payment was real, and it was not recorded when she needed it to be.

A confirmation that arrives twice. The money moved once, but a system that treats every confirmation as a new payment records it twice. Here the database is wrong even though the money did not move twice.

A system that guards against only one of these will fail on the other. The dangerous part is the same in both: the system can look successful while its records tell the wrong story.

  1. Request recordedBefore asking the payer to pay, store the request in a pending state.
  2. Provider processesThe provider handles the payment on its side.
  3. Callback arrivesIt may be late, repeated, or never come.
  4. Verify, record onceMatch it to a known request. Recording the same transaction twice must be impossible.
  5. Reconcile and informCheck stuck requests with the provider. Tell the payer the truth meanwhile.

So what should the system do?

The question changes from “did the callback say success?” to “can this transaction be processed safely, even if the same event reaches us late, twice, or not at all?” In design terms:

  • Keep a state for every payment request, such as initiated, pending, confirmed or failed, so “we are waiting” is something the system knows.
  • Verify a callback against a request you made before trusting it.
  • Make recording idempotent. Use the provider’s own transaction reference as a unique key, enforced by the database, and do the work inside one database transaction.
  • Reconcile. Periodically look for requests stuck in pending and ask the provider what happened, instead of waiting for a callback that may never come.
  • Be honest with the payer. “Payment received, confirming” is better than showing an unpaid balance that invites a second payment.

Here is the difference in code. This is illustrative pseudocode, not taken from any real system.

# Fragile: every callback becomes a payment
def on_callback(cb):
    if cb.status == "success":
        Payment.objects.create(amount=cb.amount, ref=cb.reference)

# Safer: a repeat cannot create a second record
def on_callback(cb):
    with transaction.atomic():
        req = PaymentRequest.objects.select_for_update().get(provider_id=cb.request_id)
        if req.state == "confirmed":
            return                      # a repeat: nothing more to do
        verify(cb, req)
        Payment.objects.create(request=req, provider_ref=cb.reference,
                               amount=cb.amount)   # provider_ref is UNIQUE
        req.state = "confirmed"
        req.save()

Illustrative only. A real implementation also needs authentication of the callback source, amount checks, logging and tests.

The lesson behind Mwende’s payment

The hardest bugs are not always the ones that crash an application. Some quietly corrupt the information people rely on. A student sees an unpaid balance. A parent sees money deducted. A bursar sees a ledger entry. Each sees a different fragment of the same event, and the system has to bring those fragments back into agreement.

A successful API response is an event. Trust comes from knowing what happened before it, and what the system did after it.

Mercy Mbaka
